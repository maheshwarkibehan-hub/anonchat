Chapter 3: Current Electricity

Section 3.1 to 3.4 • National Curriculum Framework

3.1 Electric Current & Current Density

Charges in motion constitute an electric current. Such currents occur naturally in many situations. Lightning is one such phenomenon in which charges flow from the clouds to the earth through the atmosphere, sometimes with disastrous results.

Current Equation:
I = ΔQ / Δt = n · A · e · vd

Where n is electron density, A is cross-sectional area, e is elementary charge (1.6 × 10-19 C), and vd is electron drift velocity.

3.2 Ohm's Law and Resistivity

A basic law regarding the flow of currents was discovered by G.S. Ohm in 1828. Imagine a conductor through which a current I is flowing and let V be the potential difference across the ends of the conductor. Then Ohm's law states that at constant temperature:

Ohm's Relation:
V ∝ I &implies; V = I · R   where R = ρ · (l / A)

3.3 Kirchhoff's Rules

Electric circuits generally consist of a number of electrical components which are connected in non-simple ways. The general rules for analyzing such networks were formulated by Gustav Kirchhoff:

  • Junction Rule (Kirchhoff's First Law): At any junction, the sum of the currents entering the junction is equal to the sum of currents leaving the junction: ∑ I = 0. This follows from conservation of electric charge.
  • Loop Rule (Kirchhoff's Second Law): The algebraic sum of changes in potential encountered in a complete traversal of any closed loop is zero: ∑ ΔV = 0. This is based on the law of conservation of energy.

3.4 Wheatstone Bridge Principle

An application of Kirchhoff's rules is the Wheatstone bridge, an arrangement of four resistances R1, R2, R3, and R4. When the bridge is balanced, no current flows through the galvanometer (Ig = 0):

Balance Condition:
R1 / R2 = R3 / R4
